Training vs forecast evaluation
In-sample fit vs out-of-sample forecast performance In-sample metrics (RMSE, R², AIC) measure how well a model reproduces the data it was fitted to. A model with 4 parameters can always fit better than one with 2 — but this does not mean it forecasts better. Overfitting is common: a highly flexible model learns noise in the training data and extrapolates it incorrectly.
The train/test split approach By fitting on weeks 1–24 and evaluating on weeks 25–28, you get an honest estimate of forecast performance. A model with slightly worse in-sample fit but better out-of-sample metrics is the preferred choice for forecasting. This is the basis of cross-validation and the philosophy behind proper scoring rules like WIS.
If your model has excellent R² in training but high forecast RMSE, it is overfitting — likely too many parameters relative to data length. Try a simpler model (logistic or Gompertz instead of Richards) or collect more data before the peak.
When forecast evaluation is not possible If your training window covers all observed data, you are fitting the full dataset. This is appropriate for final size estimation and parameter reporting — but you cannot evaluate forecast performance without held-out data. In this case, use AIC/BIC for model selection and bootstrap CIs for uncertainty.
Practical guidance For real-time forecasting during an ongoing outbreak: fit on all data available up to now, forecast forward. For retrospective evaluation: hold out the last k periods, fit on the rest, compare forecast vs observed, then refit on the full series for final estimates.
Always report whether your metrics are in-sample or out-of-sample. In-sample R² without a held-out test set is not a valid measure of forecast accuracy.
