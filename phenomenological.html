Phenomenological models

Phenomenological models describe epidemic curves using mathematical functions without assuming an underlying biological mechanism. They are fitted directly to observed case data to estimate growth, peak timing, and final size — making them powerful tools for real-time outbreak forecasting.

About phenomenological models
What is a phenomenological model? A phenomenological model describes the shape of an epidemic curve using a mathematical function chosen for its flexibility, not because it reflects biological mechanisms. The model is fitted to observed data, and parameters are estimated empirically. Examples include the logistic, Richards, and Gompertz growth models.
How does this differ from mechanistic models? Mechanistic models (SIR, SEIR) derive their equations from biological assumptions about transmission, incubation, and recovery. Phenomenological models skip these assumptions and directly fit a curve to data — making them faster to apply but less interpretable biologically.
Phenomenological models are the tool of choice for real-time forecasting when data is limited and quick projections are needed. They are less appropriate for counterfactual analyses ("what if we intervened?") — for that, use a mechanistic model.
Differential equations and epidemic growth Each phenomenological model can be expressed as a differential equation dC/dt = f(C, t, θ). This describes the instantaneous rate of change of cumulative cases C as a function of time, the current case count, and model parameters θ. Solving the DE gives the growth curve. The logistic DE is dC/dt = r·C·(1−C/K): growth is fastest when C is small (exponential phase) and slows as C approaches K (saturation).
Why does the DE form matter? The DE form reveals the biological intuition. In logistic growth, the term (1−C/K) acts as a "brake" — as cases accumulate, growth slows. The Richards model adds an asymmetry exponent to this brake, allowing faster rises than falls (or vice versa). The sub-epidemic model replaces the single brake with a sum of overlapping brakes — one per wave.
When interpreting fitted parameters, think about what the DE says: r controls how fast the curve rises, K controls where it stops, and shape parameters control the symmetry of the rise and fall.
