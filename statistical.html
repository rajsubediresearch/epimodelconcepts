Statistical models

Statistical epidemic models estimate the relationship between time and case counts using regression frameworks. Unlike phenomenological models, they make explicit distributional assumptions about the outcome — making model selection and dispersion checking essential steps.

About statistical models for epidemic data
Why not just use OLS (ordinary least squares)? Epidemic case counts are non-negative integers — they violate the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions of OLS. Count data has variance that typically increases with the mean, and OLS treats all observations equally regardless of their magnitude. GLMs (generalized linear models) are designed specifically for count data.
The GLM framework for counts All three models here are GLMs with a log link: log(E[Iₜ]) = β₀ + β₁·t. The difference lies in the assumed distribution of counts around the mean. Poisson assumes Var = Mean. Negative Binomial assumes Var = Mean + Mean²/θ, allowing variance to exceed the mean.
Always run a dispersion check (VMR) before choosing between Poisson and Negative Binomial. Using Poisson when data is overdispersed gives artificially narrow confidence intervals and inflated significance.
Interpreting regression coefficients on the log scale In log-linear and Poisson/NB regression, β₁ is the slope on the log scale. This means each unit increase in time multiplies expected cases by exp(β₁). If β₁ = 0.18 (weekly data), then exp(0.18) ≈ 1.20 — cases grow by ~20% per week. The doubling time is ln(2)/β₁.
Growth rate and doubling time β₁ is directly interpretable as the epidemic growth rate r. Doubling time = ln(2)/β₁. For COVID-19 early 2020 with weekly β₁ ≈ 0.8, doubling time ≈ 0.87 weeks ≈ 6 days — consistent with early pandemic observations.
Report exp(β₁) as the weekly/daily growth multiplier alongside the doubling time. These are more interpretable to public health audiences than the raw log-scale coefficient.
Negative Binomial dispersion parameter θ The NB dispersion parameter θ (also written k or r in some texts) controls how much extra variance is allowed beyond Poisson. When θ is large (→∞), the model approaches Poisson. When θ is small (e.g. 0.5–2), overdispersion is strong — common in outbreak data due to superspreading events, geographic clustering, and reporting heterogeneity.
What θ tells you about transmission Small θ means high heterogeneity in transmission — a few individuals or clusters drive most cases. This is the statistical signature of superspreading. In COVID-19, estimated θ values of 0.1–0.5 suggested that ~20% of cases drove ~80% of transmission.
If your fitted θ is small (<1), consider whether superspreading or spatial clustering is driving the overdispersion. This has implications for intervention design — targeting high-transmission nodes is more efficient than population-wide measures.
