About statistical models for epidemic counts
Why regression models for epidemic growth? During the exponential growth phase, log(cases) grows linearly with time. Fitting a regression on this log-linear relationship directly estimates the growth rate r = β₁ and doubling time = ln(2)/β₁. The choice of distribution (normal, Poisson, NB) affects how uncertainty is quantified.
Growth phase only Statistical regression models assume a log-linear (exponential) relationship — they cannot capture epidemic peaks or declines. Always restrict the training window to the growth phase. Forecasting into the decline phase will produce overestimates. For full-curve modeling, use phenomenological models.
A common mistake is fitting regression on the full epidemic time series. The result will be a poor fit that averages the rise and fall. The training window should end before the peak.
Negative Binomial dispersion parameter θ θ controls extra variance beyond Poisson. Large θ → Poisson. Small θ (e.g. 0.5–2) → strong overdispersion — the statistical signature of superspreading, geographic clustering, or reporting heterogeneity.
What small θ tells you In COVID-19, estimated θ ≈ 0.1–0.5 suggested ~20% of cases drove ~80% of transmission. This heterogeneity has major implications for intervention design — targeted measures on high-transmission nodes are more efficient than population-wide measures.
If fitted θ < 1, investigate whether superspreading or spatial clustering is driving the overdispersion before designing interventions.
